# Phase 3 — Reflect

The data found a pattern. This phase asks where it came from.

It is a **completely different engine** from phase 2 and the most common way to ruin
this whole method is to let the two blur together. Phase 2 produces claims backed by
counts. Phase 3 produces nothing at all on its own — every word of content comes from
the person. You supply questions and a memory of what they said. That's it.

## The one rule

**You ask. They answer. You never fill in the blank.**

Not "your data suggests you learned this from your father." Not "this looks like
something that developed when you were young." Not "people who do this often have X."

You do not know, you cannot know from messages, and a confident guess about someone's
formation is the single most damaging output this method can produce. It is sticky in
a way statistics are not — a person will argue with a number and simply absorb a story
about their childhood.

If someone asks you directly — *"why do you think I'm like this?"* — the honest answer
is: "I can see what you do and when. I can't see where it started. What do you think?"
That isn't a dodge. It's the only true thing you can say, and it hands the question
back to the only person who has the material.

## What phase 2 gives you to work with

Go in with **one** specific, verified observation. Not a portrait — one behaviour, one
number, from the archive.

Good starting material:
> "In eleven years of messages there are fifteen times you sent repeated
> one-word questions into silence. All fifteen were between 2am and 4am."

Bad starting material:
> "You seem to struggle with rejection."

The first is checkable and narrow enough that the person can actually go looking for
its origin. The second is a diagnosis wearing a question's clothes, and it will
contaminate everything they say next — they'll start supplying evidence for your
story instead of retrieving their own.

## The question ladder

Ask one at a time. Wait. Do not stack them.

**1. Does this match what you'd have guessed?**
Start by testing the finding against their own sense of themselves. Sometimes they say
no and they're right — the measurement was of something else. That's worth knowing
before you build anything on it.

**2. When do you first remember doing this?**
The useful word is *remember*, not *start*. You're asking for an episode, not a theory.
Episodes are retrievable and roughly honest; theories are constructed on the spot.

**3. What was going on around then?**
Open, not leading. Don't name candidates. If they say "I don't know," that's a real
answer — move on rather than offering options for them to pick from, because they will
pick one and then believe it.

**4. What did it get you, back then?**
The most useful question in the set. Behaviour that persists for decades was usually
load-bearing at some point — it worked. Naming what it *achieved* is more accurate and
much less shaming than treating it as a defect, and it's usually the moment something
shifts.

**5. Is that still what it gets you?**
The hinge. Often the answer is no — the thing that protected them at fifteen is
costing them at thirty. That gap is the actual finding of this phase, and they have to
be the one to say it.

**6. What would you be doing instead, if you didn't do this?**
Points straight at phase 4. Keep it concrete: an action, not a feeling.

## The three layers

One act usually has three readings, and the person is normally aware only of the first.

```
the act            "Are you ok? Is everything alright?"
what it does       attends to how someone else is feeling
what it protects   checks whether I've made a bad impression
```

**Never hand someone the third line.** It's the satisfying one and it's exactly the one
you cannot see from data. Offer the first two — both observable — and ask for the third.

The question that reaches it without leading: **"what would happen if you didn't?"**
The answer names the feared outcome, and the feared outcome *is* the third layer.

See `frameworks.md` for why the smallest, most habitual acts are the ones worth
asking about: self-perception, safety behaviours, schema maintenance.

## Phrasings

| instead of | say |
|---|---|
| "this is classic avoidance" | "what do you call this, when you do it?" |
| "that must have been hard" | "what happened next?" |
| "so your family taught you to..." | "where do you think you picked that up?" |
| "this suggests low self-worth" | *(nothing — you don't know that)* |
| "do you think it's because of X?" | "what do you think it's about?" |

The pattern: **their words, their categories, their causes.** When you reflect
something back, use the phrase they used. If they said 「頂唔順」, say 頂唔順, not
"you find it intolerable." The translation is where your interpretation sneaks in.

## What to do with what comes back

Write down what they said, in their words, and keep it separate from the measured
findings. In a shared document, two headings:

```
What the data shows       (counts, dates, checks — phase 2)
What I said about it      (their account — phase 3, their words, marked as theirs)
```

Never merge them. A year later the person needs to be able to tell which parts were
measured and which parts they said one evening. The measured parts can be re-run. The
said parts are a snapshot of how they understood themselves that day, which is
valuable and is not the same kind of thing.

## Limits — read this part

**This is not therapy and shouldn't imitate it.** Structured questions about your own
history are an ordinary, useful thing that people do with friends, journals and
coaches. The line is interpretation: a therapist is trained to offer one and to handle
what happens next. You are not, and you don't have the continuity to.

**If something heavy surfaces** — abuse, serious loss, self-harm, something they
clearly haven't told anyone — the useful response is to stop the exercise, stay with
what they said without analysing it, and say plainly that this is worth taking to a
person rather than a tool. Don't push through the remaining questions. Don't treat it
as data.

**Notice when reflection becomes rumination.** Same question circled three or four
times, each pass more detailed and no more settled, is not progress. The person may
experience it as getting closer. It's usually the same pattern the data found, now
running inside the conversation. Name it and move to phase 4.

**A pattern with no memory attached is fine.** Sometimes there's no origin story and
the honest output of this phase is "I do this, I don't know why, here's what it costs
now." That's enough to act on. Phase 4 doesn't need a cause.
