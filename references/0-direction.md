# Phase 0 — Direction

Ask where the person wants to head **before looking at any of their data.**

This phase exists because phase 4 has to sort findings into what's working and what's
costing them — and "costing" only means something against what they want. Without a
stated direction, the analyst decides what counts as a cost. That is the analyst
assembling a self again, just from the other end.

## Why before the data

Once a person has seen what they do, what they say they want quietly bends to fit it.
Someone who finds they rarely say no discovers they "never really cared about that
anyway." Someone who finds they work every night decides that's simply who they are.

So ask first, and write it down with the date, before phase 1 opens a single file. It's
the same reason a good experiment writes down what success looks like before it starts:
nobody gets to grade generously afterwards, including the person being graded.

## Direction, not destination

The danger in this phase is real. Ask someone who they want to become and they will
paint an ideal self — then measure the real one against it and fail, every day. That's a
new stick to be beaten with, and it's the *Inside Out 2* error pointed at the future: a
self assembled from a curated set of wishes instead of a curated set of memories.

So ask for **directions**, not destinations. A direction is something you face, never
somewhere you arrive — the distinction ACT (Hayes) is built on. You can head west your
whole life and never be "at west." You can only notice whether today's steps pointed
that way.

| a destination — don't | a direction — do |
|---|---|
| be more confident | say what I actually think to the people close to me |
| be a better friend | the people I love know where they stand with me |
| stop being so anxious | my evenings are mine, not my phone's |
| be like *[someone]* | *(that's someone else's — ask what it is about them)* |

The test for a good one: **could you see it in a week of their messages?** A direction
that can't show up in behaviour can't be used in phase 4.

## How to ask

Short. Two or three open questions at most, answered in the person's own words:

- *What do you want your friendships to be like?*
- *What do you want your days — or your nights — to be for?*
- *Is there something you keep meaning to be, that you'd recognise if you saw it?*

Write the answer down **verbatim**, with the date. Don't tidy it and don't translate it —
if they answered in Cantonese, it stays in Cantonese. Their words, not your summary.

If they don't know, or don't want to say, that's a complete answer. Carry on. Phase 3's
last question — *"what would you be doing instead?"* — often produces a direction later,
from the other side. And in phase 4 you then ask them what each finding costs them,
rather than deciding it yourself.

## How it's used later

**Phase 4.** A finding only goes in the *costing more than it gives* bucket if it cuts
against a direction they named. A habit that cuts against nothing they want is
load-bearing or just true — however odd it looks from outside.

**Phase 5.** The one change comes from the widest gap between a stated direction and
measured behaviour. That's what makes it theirs instead of yours.

**The gap is information, not a score.** *"You said you want your friends to know where
they stand; you decline 26% of plans without saying so"* is a finding about direction.
It is not a grade. If the person starts treating the gap as proof they've failed, say so
— the same rule as phase 4's *stop counting it as evidence*.

## It's allowed to change

A direction isn't a contract. If they come back in three months with a different one,
that isn't inconsistency — people change heading. Don't edit the old line; add a new
dated one underneath, the same way the measuring script is never edited in place:

```
2026-09-26  the people I love know where they stand with me
2026-12-30  still that — and my nights are mine
```

A list of headings over time is one of the most honest records this method produces.
