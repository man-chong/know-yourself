# The vocabulary

Words people built to describe what a person is doing and why. Use them to *name* what
the data found — never to explain it. A framework is a language, not a diagnosis, and
the difference between those two is most of this skill.

Read the evidence table at the end before presenting any of this as fact.

## Why small repeated things matter most

This is the reason to measure behaviour at all, and it deserves the top of the file.

The habits worth finding are never dramatic. They're the ones so ordinary the person
stopped seeing them — apologising, checking whether someone is annoyed, softening a
request, answering within seconds. Precisely because they're invisible, nobody has
ever examined them, and they run thousands of times.

Three mechanisms explain how something that small becomes load-bearing:

**Self-perception** (Bem, 1972). People infer their own attitudes by observing their
own behaviour, the same way they'd infer someone else's. Apologise three times a day
for ten years and the conclusion "my presence is an imposition" isn't something you
were taught — it's something you *derived*, from evidence you generated yourself. This
is the precise mechanism behind the intuition that a small act "trains" the brain.

**Safety behaviours** (from the anxiety literature). A safety behaviour is a small act
that prevents a feared outcome. Pre-emptive apology, over-explaining, answering
instantly, offering the reason before anyone asks. They work — that's the trap. Because
the feared thing never happens, the person never learns it wouldn't have happened
anyway. **The belief survives because the behaviour succeeds.** This is why "just stop
doing it" fails and why replacing it (phase 5) works better than removing it.

**Schema maintenance** (Young). A core belief stays alive through the behaviours it
produces, which then generate evidence for it. A closed loop, running on small change.

The practical consequence: when the data surfaces something tiny and constant, that's
a *stronger* finding than something rare and dramatic, not a weaker one.

## The layers under a behaviour

One act usually has three readings. They can all be true at once, and the person is
usually only aware of the first.

```
the act          "Are you ok? Is everything alright?"
what it does     attends to how someone else is feeling
what it protects checks whether I've made a bad impression
```

Or:

```
the act          saying sorry
what it does     smooths a moment
what it protects keeps me from taking up space I'm not sure I'm allowed
```

**Never hand someone the third line as a finding.** That's phase 3's rule (including its
one narrow exception — a guess the data can test, with the test attached), and it matters most here,
because the third line is the satisfying one and it is exactly the one you cannot see
from data. Offer the first two, which are observable, and ask what the third is.

The question that gets there without leading: **"what would happen if you didn't?"**
The answer names the feared outcome, and the feared outcome is the layer underneath.

## Depth — where it came from

**Jung.** The **Shadow** — the disowned parts, recognisable because they irritate you
disproportionately in other people. The **persona**, the face assembled for the world.
**Archetypes**, including the *divine child*, which became the **inner child** of the
1980s via John Bradshaw. **Individuation**: becoming whole by integrating the shadow
rather than defeating it.

**Freud.** The unconscious. **Defence mechanisms** — repression, projection,
rationalisation, displacement, reaction formation, sublimation. The vocabulary
survived far better than the theory.

**Internal Family Systems** (Schwartz). The modern, usable version: you are made of
**parts**. *Exiles* carry the young pain. *Managers* run your life to keep exiles from
surfacing — perfectionism, people-pleasing, over-preparing. *Firefighters* put out the
feeling when an exile breaks through. No part is an enemy, including the ones you
dislike; each took its job for a reason.

**Transactional Analysis** (Berne). Three ego states — Parent, Adult, Child — and
**games**: repeating exchanges with a predictable bad ending that both people keep
walking into. Also **scripts**, the life-story decided early and then lived out. If
someone describes being "hooked" into the same argument, this is the vocabulary.

## Attachment — how you love

Bowlby and Ainsworth; extended to adults by Hazan & Shaver. Four patterns:

| | |
|---|---|
| **secure** | closeness is fine, distance is fine |
| **anxious** | distance reads as danger; protest behaviour follows |
| **avoidant** | closeness reads as danger; distance restores safety |
| **disorganised** | both at once — approach and flee |

**Protest behaviour** is the technical term for what repeated unanswered messages
are. Naming it matters because it's a normal attachment response, not a character
flaw, and because the anxious/avoidant pairing explains more relationship dynamics
than anything else in this file.

Best-evidenced of the origin frameworks. Also the most over-applied — people type
themselves and their exes and stop looking.

## Cognitive — what you tell yourself

**Cognitive triangle**: situation → thought → feeling → behaviour. The intervention
point is the thought, because it's the only link you can catch in motion.

**Cognitive distortions** (Beck, Burns): catastrophising · mind-reading ·
**personalisation** (assuming it's about you) · fortune-telling · all-or-nothing ·
emotional reasoning (it feels true so it is) · discounting the positive.

**Core beliefs** sit under the distortions: *I'm not enough. I'm too much. I'll be
left.* **Schema Therapy** (Young) catalogues eighteen — abandonment,
defectiveness/shame, subjugation, emotional deprivation, unrelenting standards — each
with the coping styles that keep it alive: surrender, avoidance, overcompensation.

## Behavioural — why it persists

The most underrated lens and often the most useful.

**Functional analysis (ABC)**: Antecedent → Behaviour → Consequence. Anything a person
has done for twenty years is being **reinforced** — it is getting them something, even
if the something is only the temporary end of a bad feeling. **Negative reinforcement**
(removing discomfort) is far stickier than reward, and it's what most self-criticised
habits run on.

The question this lens asks is not *what's wrong with me* but **what is this doing for
me.** That reframe alone is worth the whole field, and it's far more accurate as well
as far less shaming.

## Motivation — what you're actually after

**Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan). Three needs, well supported:
**autonomy** (I chose this), **competence** (I'm good at this), **relatedness** (I
belong). Most persistent unhappiness maps to one of them being starved.

**ACT** (Hayes) treats **values** as directions rather than goals — you never arrive,
you only face that way. Plus *cognitive defusion*: the difference between "I'm a
failure" and "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure."

Maslow's hierarchy is the famous one and the weakest; the pyramid was drawn by someone
else and the levels don't behave like levels.

## Self-concept

**Self-efficacy** (Bandura) is task-specific — high at building things, low at being
chosen, in the same person, with no contradiction. Most talk about "confidence"
collapses these and gets confused.

**Self-compassion** (Neff): self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness. Beats
self-esteem on nearly every outcome, because it doesn't require being above average.

**Imposter phenomenon** (Clance & Imes, 1978) — attributing your results to luck or
timing, and expecting exposure.

## Narrative — the story you tell

**Narrative identity** (McAdams). People default to **redemption** sequences (bad
turned into meaning) or **contamination** sequences (good ruined by what followed).
Which one someone reaches for predicts a great deal.

**Narrative therapy** (White & Epston): *externalising* — the problem is the problem,
the person is not the problem. Useful language when a finding starts being treated as
an identity.

**Mood-congruent recall** — the mechanism that makes self-concept self-confirming.
People low in mood retrieve low-mood memories more readily, which supports the low
self-assessment, which sustains the mood. A self built at 3am is built from a filtered
archive and is honest about the filter's contents rather than about the person.

This is the evidenced version of *Inside Out 2*'s Sense of Self: the self grows from
whichever memories reach it, so whoever controls the selection controls the self —
and both the cheerful curation and the anxious one are false in exactly the same way.
It is also the strongest argument for measuring rather than remembering. A saved
script reads the whole archive; memory reads whatever matches today's mood.

## Traits

**Big Five / OCEAN** — openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness,
neuroticism. The only trait model with serious evidence; describes *how much* of
something, never *why*. **HEXACO** adds honesty–humility.

Traits describe. They don't explain and they don't prescribe.

## Interpersonal

**Johari window** — four panes: open (you and others know), hidden (only you),
**blind spot** (others see it, you don't), unknown. This method operates almost
entirely on the blind-spot pane, which is why the findings feel like news.

**Karpman drama triangle** — victim, rescuer, persecutor, and the way people rotate
between all three inside one conflict.

**Differentiation** (Bowen) — staying yourself while staying connected.

## Contemplative and non-Western

**Buddhism**: *anattā*, no fixed self — a direct challenge to this whole exercise,
worth taking seriously. There may be no stable thing to find, only patterns that
arise. *Vipassanā* trains observing a feeling without acting on it, which is the skill
phase 4 is actually asking for.

**Stoicism**: the dichotomy of control. *Amor fati* — not tolerating what happened but
being willing to have it.

**Chinese systems**: 紫微斗數, 八字, 五行, 陰陽. These work as narrative and as a
vocabulary for temperament, and are honest about not being measurement. Where a person
already uses them, use their terms — the language they think in beats a translated one.

**Ikigai**, **wabi-sabi** — the second is more useful here: worth in the incomplete and
the imperfect, which is the frame this method needs when a finding is unflattering.

## How much to trust each

| well supported | useful language, not testable | popular, weak |
|---|---|---|
| Big Five · attachment · CBT & distortions · self-determination · self-compassion · reinforcement · ACEs · self-perception · safety behaviours | Jung's shadow · IFS parts · inner child · transactional analysis · narrative identity · schema therapy · the contemplative traditions | MBTI · Enneagram · Maslow's pyramid · learning styles · most of "left brain / right brain" · much of growth mindset after replication |

The middle column is not worthless. A frame doesn't have to be falsifiable to help
someone think. It just must not be presented as measurement — which is MBTI's actual
problem: not that it's meaningless, but that it's sold as a test when it's a vocabulary.

**When you use any of this, say which column it's from.**
